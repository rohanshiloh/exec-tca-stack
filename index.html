Why Measure a Fill

A trilogy. Part One, The Order & Trade Lifecycle: the map. Part Two, Anatomy of a Fill: the territory. Part Three, Sharded Ticks: the machine.

Companion. The Order & Trade Lifecycle: where the order comes from and where the fill goes — the OMS, the EMS, what an order really becomes and how a parent is sliced.Part One follows an instruction from the investment decision through routing, execution, clearing and settlement. This part starts where that one ends, at the fill, and asks the question that is left once a trade has happened: was it a good one? Part Three is the infrastructure that runs this at scale.

The whole argument in one paragraph. Every benchmark here is the same question asked with a different reference price: compared with what? Arrival compares the fill with the mid when the order reached the desk. VWAP and TWAP compare it with an average over a window. A markout compares it with the mid a few seconds later. Computing any of them is a time-correct lookup of that reference price, and in kdb+ that is an as-of join or a window aggregate over tables you already have. The rest of the document is that idea, worked through.

Transaction cost analysis — TCA — answers a plain question: when you bought or sold a stock, did you get a good price, and if not, where did the money go? Trading isn't free even when nobody charges a commission, because the act of buying nudges the price up against you. This section builds the idea from the ground up — order, book, spread, fill, benchmark — so the sharper terms in the rest of the document have something to stand on. The headline it all leads to: the spread you crossed is the small, visible part of the cost; the price that drifted while you worked the order is usually the larger, invisible one.

1An Order Trades in Pieces

An order is just an instruction: buy — or sell — a certain number of shares of one stock. A large order (traders call it the parent)Parent & child. The parent is the whole order; children are the slices actually sent to market. One parent → many children → many fills, all tied together by a parent id. almost never trades all at once, because pushing 100,000 shares into the market in one go would shove the price against you. Instead a system cuts it into many small child orders and releases them over seconds, minutes or hours. Each child that actually trades comes back as a fill — a receipt saying "you got this many shares at this price." Every trade that prints also lands on a public record called the tape — the trades half of what the industry calls TAQ data (Trades and Quotes)TAQ. Originally NYSE’s Trade-and-Quote dataset; now the generic name for combined trade + quote data. In the US the consolidated feeds (the SIP) carry the public tape.; the quotes are the other half.Four levels. Parent, algo order, child, fill. The trader hands the parent to one or more broker algos; each algo order is one algo’s share of it, with its own start, pace and settings. The algo slices it into children and its router places them. Keeping all four ids is what lets you separate what the algo cost from what the router cost.

2The Book and the Spread

Buyers and sellers advertise their prices in an order book. The highest price any buyer will currently pay is the bid; the lowest price any seller will accept is the ask (or offer)BBO / NBBO. The best bid and offer on one venue is the BBO; the best across all US venues is the National BBO — the consolidated top of book you’re measured against.. The small gap between them is the spread, and the point halfway between is the mid — the nearest thing to one "fair price right now."Mid. Usually not a price you can actually trade at — it is a reference. Midpoint dark pools are the exception: they fill you exactly there, splitting the spread. To buy immediately you must pay the ask — you cross the spread — and to sell immediately you take the bid. That gap is the first and smallest cost of trading.

Not every venue shows you its book, and the three kinds leave different marks on a fillLit, dark, internalised. Lit venues (ARCA, NASDAQ, BATS, EDGX here) publish their bids and offers, which is what makes a consolidated best price possible. Dark pools display nothing and match out of sight, usually at the mid. An internaliser is your own broker taking the other side from its own book. — which is why execs carries a liq code recording which one happened. It is the difference between paying the spread, splitting it, and never touching a public venue at all.

3What a Fill Really Costs

Crossing the spread is the cost you can see. The bigger, hidden cost is drift:Temporary vs permanent. Some drift fades once you stop pushing (temporary impact); some stays, because your trading revealed information (permanent). Almgren–Chriss trade this against timing risk. while you patiently work a large order, the price tends to move away from you — partly because your own buying pushes it up, partly because others sense the pressure. By the time you finish, your average fill can sit well above the price when you started. That starting price — the mid at the moment the order arrived — is the arrival price, and the distance from it to your average fill is the headline number of TCA: the implementation shortfall.Perold (1988). Named for the gap between a “paper” portfolio that trades instantly at the decision price and the real one that pays to get there.

That drift has sharper names you'll meet later — market impact (the move you cause), adverse selection and venue toxicity (trading right before the price moves against you) — but they are all the same plain idea: the price you end up with is worse than the price you first saw. The whole stack exists to pin down how much worse, and why.

Only the first row is explicit, printed on an invoice. The rest are implicit, and the most expensive of them are the hardest to see. Those four implicit names get conflated constantly, including by people who should know better. They are different phenomena with different causes, and the fix for each is different:

4A Benchmark Is Just a Yardstick

You can't call a price "good" or "bad" on its own — you need something to compare it against. A benchmark is a reference price standing in for "what a fair execution should have cost," and slippage is how far your average price landed from it. Slippage is quoted in basis points — bps, where one bp is 0.01%, so 5 bps on a $100 stock is half a cent a share.

The benchmarks differ only in which reference price they pick: the price when you started (arrival), the market's own volume-weighted average over your trading window (VWAP), the closing price (close), or the price a moment after each fill (a markout, which catches whether you were picked off). Later we line these up by when in the trade's life each one looks — but every one is just a single comparison.

5From Measurement to a Loop

Three audiences read the same numbers for different reasons. A buy-side desk uses TCA to choose algos and venues and to evidence best execution. A sell-side desk uses it to tune its router and defend its fills. Compliance uses it as the trail behind a best-execution policy. Despite the different goals they all lean on the same mechanism: compare the price you actually achieved against a benchmark chosen to suit the question being asked. Which benchmark you pick is the question.

It runs in three phases that form a loop. Pre-trade: estimate the cost, mostly from the order's size relative to ADV,ADV. Average daily volume — how much the stock trades on a normal day. It is the yardstick for “is this order big?”: 200,000 shares of a 4-million-share name is 5% of ADV, a real order. and pick a strategy. In-flight: watch participation — your POVPOV. Percentage of volume: your shares as a fraction of everything printing in the same window. At 10% POV you aim to be one share in every ten, speeding up when the tape is busy. — and slippage against that estimate while the order works. Post-trade: attribute the realised cost by benchmark and venue, then feed what you learned back into the cost model and the routing table. The post-trade scorecard isn't a report you file — it is the input to tomorrow's pre-trade.

One Sign Convention

Every benchmark in this document returns basis points signed so that positive is favourable to the order: a buy that paid below the benchmark, a sell that sold above it. The sign lives in one helper, .b.buyorsell, which is +1 for a buy and -1 for a sell. Bps are 1e4 * (price difference) % reference, so a markout of -2.5 on a buy means the mid fell two and a half basis points against you after the fill.

There are two exceptions, both measuring a cost paid, where positive means cost: the spread measures in .b.spreads (effective, realised, impact), which follow the sign every microstructure paper uses, and the fee measures in .b.feeAware (spread paid, fee, all-in). Their column names say so and the rest of the document does not repeat this.

Why This Lives in kdb+

Every number in TCA is a join against time: each fill to the quote that prevailed at that instant, each order to the volume on the tape across its window, each placement to the book as it stood when the router decided. That is aj — the as-of join — over a columnar history of hundreds of millions of events a day. The measurement is the query, which is why the same handful of lines run live on today's stream and in batch over a year of partitions, and why point-in-time correctness (§Benchmarks) is the default rather than a feature.

Why a Chained Tickerplant

A tickerplant is single-threaded: it logs each message, then publishes it to every subscriber in turn. If one subscriber is slow to drain, the TP's output queue to it grows and, past -w, the TP blocks — and now everything downstream is late, including the RDB that the trading desk is querying.

TCA is exactly the kind of subscriber that misbehaves: an analyst runs a 60-second markout over the whole day, the query takes two seconds, and for those two seconds the CEP isn't reading its socket. Put it behind a chained TP (chainedtick.q, no log, pure fan-out) and the blast radius is contained to the chain. The main plant never feels it.

One Quote Is Not the NBBO

The biggest simplification in this stack, stated plainly: updTop in engine.q takes the last quote in the batch as each symbol's reference mid. Real desks mark against the NBBO — the National Best Bid and Offer, the highest bid and lowest ask across all lit venues at that instant — which in q is select nbb:max bid, nbo:min ask by sym from quote where venue in lit, maintained per update rather than per batch.

Everything downstream inherits this: arrival mid, markouts, effective and realised spread all read that one number. With six venues quoting a 2-cent spread the error is small and unbiased, so the labs still teach what they are meant to teach. On a real tape it is neither small nor unbiased — a venue quoting through the NBBO would flatter or damn itself by setting its own benchmark. Fix this first when you point the stack at real data; it is a dozen lines, and nothing else in bench.q has to change.

Internalisation Is Scored Apart

A fill tagged liq="I" never touched the street: the broker crossed it against its own book at the mid. It carries no spread cost and, by construction, no venue markout — so if you pool it with lit fills it quietly flatters the blended number and hides whatever the lit routing is doing.

Keep it in its own bucket. The scorecard groups by venue, and INTL is just another row; read it next to the lit venues, never merged into them. The question internalisation answers is a different one — was the mid fair? — not did we capture spread?

Data flows left to right: feed.q publishes to the TP, which logs every message and fans it out to the RDB and to a chained TP; tca.q loads engine.q behind that chain and turns bench.q into prompt commands. replay.q and rescue.q work on the TP log off to the side; lab.q is the scratch driver; beyond.q adds the Beyond-Vanilla benchmarks and dump.q writes a diagnostics file. The one idea that keeps it small: the measurement is the query, so there is no separate analytics code — bench.q is the product.

TP:`$":",$[count .z.x; first .z.x; ":5010"];
h:neg hopen TP;                              / async handle to the tickerplant
pub:{h(`.u.upd;x;y)};                        / feeds call .u.upd on the TP; y = columns, no time

/ all top-level (no namespace): KDB-X does not fall back to root for reads
n:count uni;
mid:value refPx;                             / live mid per sym, aligned to uni
prevMid:mid;                                 / the mid one tick ago: resting orders fill on the move
fastD:n#0f; slowD:n#0f;                       / venue after-effects, two speeds (bps pools)
impD:n#0f;                                    / our own market impact, decaying (bps)
vol:0.00001+0.000004*til n;                   / per-step volatility per sym (~0.1-0.4 bps)
/ After each fill a venue moves the mid AGAINST the trader by these amounts (bps):
/ fast = within a second (someone was quicker), slow = over about a minute
/ (someone knew more). Centred over the lit venues, so that an algo's own later
/ fills are not helped or hurt by its own routing; the dark venues leak nothing.
toxF:venues!(-0.4 -0.4 -0.4 0.8 0.0 0.0);    / EDGX: damage inside the first second
toxS:venues!(-0.5 -0.5 0.45 0.05 0.0 0.0);   / BATS: damage that keeps arriving
alpha:1.5;                                    / the decision's signal: bps the price drifts the order's way, on average
impK:0.02; impDecay:0.85;                     / impact per 100 shares, and how fast it partly fades per tick
pool:`ARCA`NSDQ`BATS`EDGX`EDGX`BATS`MLIT`INTL; / default router mix (lit-heavy, some dark/house)
litPool:`ARCA`NSDQ`BATS`EDGX`EDGX`BATS;        / sweep the lit book only
darkPool:`MLIT`MLIT`INTL;                      / midpoint and house only
/ the four broker algos the wheel chooses between, and how each one behaves
/   pTrade   share of ticks it sends a child     sMin,sRange  child size, in lots
/   pPassive share of lit children that rest      route        which venue pool
algoParams:([algo:`VWAP`POV`ARRIVAL`DARK]
  pTrade:0.5 0.8 1.0 0.25; sMin:1 1 3 2; sRange:3 4 6 5;
  pPassive:0.45 0.3 0.0 0.0; route:`pool`pool`lit`dark);
algoNames:exec algo from 0!algoParams;
lot:100; seq:0; nTick:0;                       / nTick counts timer ticks (100ms each)
nid:{seq+:1; `$x,string seq};                 / monotonic id with a prefix
rnd:{-1f+2f*x?1f};                            / x draws, uniform on (-1,1)

/ ── market data ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
/ Each fill adds its venue's toxicity (bps) to the two drift pools. Those pools
/ decay, so a pool of D bps must be released as D*(1-decay) per step — otherwise
/ the same displacement is re-applied every tick and the price runs away.
/ NOTE the `::` — inside a lambda a plain `x:` binds a LOCAL, so the clamp was
/ silently doing nothing. (`x*:` and `x[i]+:` DO amend the global; `x:` does not.)
/ Decay and clamp are folded into one global assignment each.
mdStep:{ prevMid::mid;
  fastD::(-12f)|12f&0.55*fastD;               / fast decays sub-second, then clamp
  slowD::(-12f)|12f&0.99*slowD;               / slow decays over ~7s, then clamp
  rev:impD*impDecay-1; impD+:rev;             / impact fades: the price gives some back
  drift:1e-4*(0.45*fastD)+(0.01*slowD)+rev;   / per-step share of the move
  pull:0.0005*(value refPx)-mid;              / gentle pull to the anchor px
  mid+:pull+(mid*vol*rnd n)+(mid*drift); };

pushQuotes:{ nv:count lit; r:n*nv;
  sy:raze nv#'uni; vv:r#lit; m:raze nv#'mid;  / sym-major rows, lit cycled within each sym
  hs:0.01*1+r?3; vj:0.005*rnd r;              / 1–3c half-spread, small per-venue offset
  pub[`quote;(sy; vv; m-hs+vj; m+hs+vj; lot*1+r?15; lot*1+r?15)]; };

pushTape:{ k:10+rand 20; ix:k?n;              / 10–29 background prints near the touch
  pub[`trade;(uni ix; pool k?count pool; (mid ix)+0.005*rnd k; lot*1+k?10)]; };

/ ── order / execution flow ──────────────────────────────────────────────
/ parent (the PM's order) -> algo orders (one per broker algo) -> children -> fills.
/ The feed works ALGO orders; a parent is just the sum of its algo orders.
/ limitPx is null for a market order; born is the tick it arrived on.
active:([algoOrderID:`symbol$()] parentOrderID:`symbol$(); sym:`symbol$(); side:`char$();
  algo:`symbol$(); qty:`long$(); filledQty:`long$(); limitPx:`float$(); born:`long$());

spawnParent:{ if[0.08<first 1?1f; :()];       / ~8% of ticks start a parent
  s:first 1?n; sd:$[0.5>first 1?1f;"B";"S"]; sg:$[sd="B";1f;-1f];
  q:lot*10*1+first 1?40; p:nid"P";
  / 30% of parents carry a limit 1-3 bps through the arrival mid; the rest are market
  isLim:0.3>first 1?1f; ot:$[isLim;"L";"M"];
  lim:$[isLim; (mid s)*1+1e-4*sg*1+first 1?2f; 0n];
  / a parent carries a small directional signal: the price tends to drift its way
  / over the next half minute. That is decision quality, separate from execution.
  @[`slowD; s; +; sg*alpha*first 1?2f];
  / the parent, as the OMS records it: no algo yet, no child
  pub[`ord;(enlist uni s; enlist p; enlist `; enlist `; enlist `; enlist sd; enlist ot;
            enlist q; enlist lim; enlist "0"; enlist `; enlist `NEW)];
  / the trader hands it to one or two DIFFERENT broker algos, picked at random.
  / Random assignment reduces selection bias and gives each algo a comparable
  / sample of flow; enough of it is what makes the comparison meaningful.
  q1:lot*floor 0.6*q%lot;                      / a 60/40 split, in whole lots
  qs:$[0.6>first 1?1f; enlist q; (q1; q-q1)];
  k:count qs; ids:nid'[k#"A"]; al:algoNames neg[k]?count algoNames;   / neg: deal, no repeats
  `active upsert ([] algoOrderID:ids; parentOrderID:k#p; sym:k#uni s; side:k#sd; algo:al;
    qty:qs; filledQty:k#0; limitPx:k#lim; born:k#nTick);
  pub[`ord;(k#uni s; k#p; ids; al; k#`; k#sd; k#ot; qs; k#lim; k#"0"; k#`SOR; k#`NEW)]; };

workOrders:{ if[0=count active; :()];
  a:0!active;
  / a limited order only trades while the touch is inside its limit
  m0:mid uni?a`sym; isBuy:(a`side)="B";
  a:a where (null a`limitPx) | ?[isBuy; (m0+0.01)<=a`limitPx; (m0-0.01)>=a`limitPx];
  a:a lj algoParams;                            / each algo order picks up its algo's behaviour
  a:a where (count[a]?1f)<a`pTrade;             / and only trades on its share of ticks
  if[0=count a; :()];
  c:count a; si:uni?a`sym; sg:?[(a`side)="B"; 1f; -1f]; r:a`route;
  vn:?[r=`lit; litPool c?count litPool; ?[r=`dark; darkPool c?count darkPool; pool c?count pool]];
  passive:(vn in lit) & (c?1f)<a`pPassive;
  / A resting order fills mainly when the market comes to it: here a resting buy is
  / hit as the price falls or holds, never as it rises. That makes adverse selection
  / possible, where filling every resting order by construction would hide it; the
  / rebate is what a venue pays you to accept it. (Real queues also fill through
  / queue advance and contra flow; this feed keeps only the price-driven part.)
  k:where not passive & 0<sg*(mid si)-prevMid si;
  if[0=count k; :()];
  a:a k; vn:vn k; passive:passive k; si:si k; sg:sg k; c:count k;
  slice:(a[`qty]-a`filledQty) & lot*a[`sMin]+floor a[`sRange]*c?1f;   / capped at remaining
  takePx:(mid si)+0.01*sg;                      / take: pay the far touch
  makePx:(prevMid si)-0.01*sg;                  / make: the near touch you were resting on
  px:?[passive; makePx; ?[vn in lit; takePx; mid si]];   / dark / house fill at the mid
  liq:?[vn in lit; ?[passive; "A"; "R"]; ?[vn=house; "I"; "M"]];
  / R removed lit, A added lit, I internalised, M dark midpoint.
  / functional amend: accumulates when two orders hit the same sym in one tick
  @[`fastD; si; +; neg sg*toxF vn];             / the venue's after-effect, against the trader
  @[`slowD; si; +; neg sg*toxS vn];
  imp:sg*impK*slice%lot;                        / our own impact: moves the price now, then partly fades
  @[`impD; si; +; imp]; @[`mid; si; *; 1+1e-4*imp];
  cum:a[`filledQty]+slice; lv:a[`qty]-cum;      / per algo order, mirroring FIX CumQty / LeavesQty
  pub[`execs;(a`sym; a`parentOrderID; a`algoOrderID; a`algo; nid'[c#"C"]; a`side; c#"F";
             px; slice; lv; cum; vn; liq)];
  pub[`trade;(a`sym; vn; px; slice)];           / our own fills print on the tape too
  `active upsert (cols active)#update filledQty:cum from a;   / write progress back by key
  if[count fin:a[`algoOrderID] where lv<=0; delete from `active where algoOrderID in fin]; };

/ ── the cost curve ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
/ Realised cost against order size as a share of the day's volume, in k buckets,
/ next to the square-root model with fitted constant C. Built on demand from three
/ live tables: parent (arrivalMid, stamped from quote when the order arrived), execs
/ (the fills) and trade (the tape, for ADV and sigma). Nothing is stored.
.b.costCurve:{[a;trade;C;k]
  sq:select adv:sum size, sigma:dev 1_deltas log px by sym from trade;
  a:select sym, costBps:neg slipBps, Q:filled from a where filled>0;
  a:update z:Q%adv, pred:1e4*sigma*sqrt Q%adv from a lj sq;
  0!select n:count i, pctADV:100*avg z, costBps:avg costBps, se:(dev costBps)%sqrt count i,
      modelBps:C*avg pred by bucket:k xrank z from a };

/ ── F12 the broker report ────────────────────────────────────────────────
/ The columns a retail broker's TCA page shows, per parent: price improvement
/ against the far touch at arrival, cost against the day's VWAP, how much of the
/ day's high-low range the order captured, how much of it the touch could have
/ filled at once, and how long it lived.
.b.brokerReport:{[parent;execs;quote;trade]

  p:aj[`sym`time; p; select time,sym,bid,ask,bsize,asize from quote];

/ ── four prices, four costs ──────────────────────────────────────────────
/ Perold's shortfall split at the four moments EDHEC names: the decision (P0,
/ the mid lag before the parent arrived), the desk's release to the broker (PR,
/ the parent's arrivalMid), the broker starting work (PS, the qty-weighted
/ arrivalMid of its algo orders) and the end (PT). + = favourable, all in bps of P0.
/ ops + timing are charged on every share, execution on the filled ones and
/ opportunity on the unfilled ones; the four always sum to Perold's total, isBps.
.b.shortfall4:{[parent;algoOrd;execs;quote;lag;closes]
  a:.b.arrival[parent;execs];
  d:aj[`sym`time; select sym, time:arrivalTime-lag from a; select time,sym,bid,ask from quote];
  a:update decisionMid:0.5*d[`bid]+d`ask from a;
  a:a lj select startMid:qty wavg arrivalMid by parentOrderID from algoOrd;
  a:update phi:.b.buyorsell side, endPx:closes sym from a;
  a:update opsBps:1e4*phi*(decisionMid-arrivalMid)%decisionMid,
           timingBps:1e4*phi*(arrivalMid-startMid)%decisionMid,
           execBps:?[fillRate>0; fillRate*1e4*phi*(startMid-averageFillPrice)%decisionMid; 0f],
           oppBps:(1-fillRate)*1e4*phi*(startMid-endPx)%decisionMid,
           isBps:(?[fillRate>0; fillRate*1e4*phi*(decisionMid-averageFillPrice)%decisionMid; 0f])
                 +(1-fillRate)*1e4*phi*(decisionMid-endPx)%decisionMid from a;
  select parentOrderID, sym, side, fillRate, decisionMid, arrivalMid, startMid, averageFillPrice,
    endPx, opsBps, timingBps, execBps, oppBps, isBps from a };

/ ── I-star: Kissell and Glantz's impact model ────────────────────────────
/ Instantaneous impact I = a1 * Z^a2 * sigma^a3 (bps), Z = size as % of ADV,
/ sigma = daily volatility in bps; about 95% of it is temporary and fades with
/ slower trading. pov = order size / market volume over the horizon, so the
/ participation number eta = 1 + 0.5/pov. Defaults: EDHEC's power-function fit.
.b.istar:{[Z;sigmaBps;pov]
  i:25*(Z xexp 0.38)*sigmaBps xexp 0.28;
  i*0.05+0.95%1+0.5%pov };

    vsMidHL:f[phi;midHL;averageFillPrice], vsClose:f[phi;close;averageFillPrice] from a };

Average fill against the mid at the moment the order arrived. This is the most complete measure of cost — it includes how far the price moved while you were trading, not just the spread you paid. Decomposes into delay, execution and opportunity cost.

VWAP · TWAP · PWP · Close

Schedule benchmarks. VWAP asks whether you beat the tape over your window; TWAP whether you beat a clock; PWP whether you beat a fixed share of volume; close whether you beat the last print. Easy to game by choosing the window.

Markouts & Spread Capture

The only forward-looking family: where did the mid go after you traded? Effective spread is what you paid; realised spread is what you kept once the market moved; the difference is impact. This is where venue toxicity shows up.

P

PWP & the Achievable Range

Participation-weighted price measures in volume-time rather than clock-time: for a 20% target, the yardstick is the VWAP of the first Q/0.20 shares the market printed after you started. "+2 bps vs PWP" means nothing until you know whether the range was 4 bps wide or 40.

O

Opportunity Cost

The shares you never traded have a cost too, and it is invisible in every other benchmark on this page. Weight the drift from arrival to your end price by the fraction you failed to fill. An order that got a beautiful price on 30% of its size, while the other 70% ran away, is not a good execution — this is the only number that says so.

Arrival ignores the unfilled remainder's drift unless you add opportunity cost. VWAP rewards trading when everyone trades. Markouts need clean quotes that extend past your last fill. Read the fillRate next to every slippage number.

Point-in-Time, or It's Fiction

A benchmark is honest only if its reference price uses what was knowable at that instant — the quote at or before the fill, never the one after. Score a 10:00:00 fill against a 10:00:02 mid and you have smuggled two seconds of hindsight into the number. Vendors build whole engines around this point-in-time discipline;7 in kdb+ it is simply the semantics of aj, which for each fill takes the last quote at or before its time. Look-ahead isn't something you prevent here — you would have to go out of your way to introduce it.

The same rule forbids rebuilding the book after the fact: the at-placement snapshot — depth, queue-ahead, OFI — must be captured in-band, because an afterwards view shows the book that resulted, not the one the router acted on (§C3).

Four Prices, Four Costs

B1's shortfall has one starting price. EDHEC's survey9 uses four, each stamped at a moment someone handed the order on, so the cost can be charged to whoever held it. The portfolio manager decides at \(P_0\). The desk releases the order to the broker at \(P_R\). The broker starts working it at \(P_S\). Trading ends at \(P_T\). Each gap is a different person's cost: operational delay (\(P_0\to P_R\)) belongs to the desk's workflow, timing (\(P_R\to P_S\)) to the broker's queue, spread and impact (\(P_S\to\bar P\)) to the execution, and opportunity (\(P_S\to P_T\) on the shares never filled) to whoever set the limit or cancelled.

This stack has all four prices already. \(P_R\) is the parent's arrivalMid; \(P_S\) is its algo orders' arrivalMid, weighted by their size; \(P_T\) is the close. The decision is the one price a TCA system never sees, because it happens in the PM's head, so here it is the mid five seconds before the parent arrived. Parents that arrive in the first five seconds of a session have no decision mid (there is no quote yet), so their rows are null and drop out of the averages. In the simulator the parent and its algo orders arrive on the same tick, so timing is zero; in a real desk, where an order can sit in an OMS queue for minutes, it often is not.

Reading a Markout Curve

Computing a markout is one join. Reading one takes more care, and there are four things to look at, in this order.

The level at the shortest horizon. This is what the fill cost you before anything else happened. A taker starts about half a spread down, because it paid to cross; a resting order starts about half a spread up, because it was paid to wait. In the live run the taker fills sit at −0.42 bps after one second and the passive fills at +0.21. Compare venues within the same kind of fill, or the mix of making and taking will pass for venue quality.

The slope from one horizon to the next. A curve that drops and then goes flat was a one-off: someone was faster, the damage is done, and it does not grow. EDGX in the live scorecard is −0.73, −0.68, −0.75. A curve that starts near zero and keeps falling is information arriving after you traded: BATS runs −0.12, −0.29, −0.49. The first is a latency problem and the second a routing problem, and one blended number cannot tell them apart.

The sign at the longest horizon. Positive and rising means the market kept moving your way after you traded, which usually reflects your own remaining order still pushing the price. Negative means you would have done better by waiting. Near zero means you paid the spread and nothing else.

The number of fills behind each point. A sixty-second markout is noisy: over ten minutes of this feed it moved by a few tenths of a basis point between runs, and settled only at thirty. Put the count beside every average, and prefer the median when the two disagree.

Why the Toxic Venue Fills You First

A fill needs someone on the other side, and the question a scorecard exists to answer is who that was. On a venue where the other side is mostly slow, uninformed flow, your resting order waits its turn and fills at a price that stays fair. On a venue where the other side is fast or informed, your order fills at the moment it becomes a bad price: just before the quote moves through it. From the inside those look the same, except that the second kind fills sooner and more often.

That is why fill rate is a dangerous way to rank venues. Availability and quality pull in opposite directions: the venue most willing to trade with you right now is disproportionately the one whose counterparties know something you do not. Rank on fill rate and you route more flow to exactly the place that costs you most.

It is also what rebates are for. A venue pays you to rest an order because resting is a risk: you are offering a free option to anyone faster than you. The rebate is the premium for writing that option, set by a venue that knows how often it gets exercised. In the fee lab (F2), BATS pays the best rebate on the street, 0.25 bps, and its one-minute markout takes back 0.49.

So read the scorecard as a row. Fill rate says how available a venue is, the short markout how fast its counterparties are, the long markout how informed they are, and the fee line what you were paid to find out.

Market Impact: How Far You Move It

Every buy is a small piece of evidence that someone wants the stock. The book absorbs it by moving: the ask you lifted is gone, the next one is higher, and the participants watching the tape revise up. That move is market impact, and it is the one cost in the taxonomy that you cause rather than pay to someone. Part of it is temporary, liquidity that refills once you stop pushing, and part is permanent, because your trading told the market something. The markout curve in C1 is where the split shows: the drop from the one-second to the one-minute markout is the temporary part going away.

The desk's model for its size is the square-root law: impact grows with volatility and with the square root of the order's share of the day's volume, not in proportion to it. Doubling the order raises the cost by about 40%, not 100%.

\[ \text{impact}_{\text{bps}} \;\approx\; 10^{4}\cdot C\,\sigma\,\sqrt{\frac{Q}{\text{ADV}}} \]

Here \(\sigma\) is daily volatility, \(Q\) the order size, ADV the average daily volume, and \(C\) a constant of order one that you fit to your own flow (D2). It describes a meta-order worked over the day, not a single fill, and it measures the move you would see if nothing else happened, which is never true. So test it on parents, not fills: bucket parents by size, and check that realised cost rises like the square root of size rather than like size itself.

Two things show up once there are enough parents. The first is that realised cost does rise with size, from 0.44 to about 0.7 bps across the smaller half of the orders, and then levels off. The feed's impact is charged per slice and fades within a second, and every parent here is under 0.02% of the session's volume, so the rise is short and the plateau comes early; real impact accumulates over a parent's whole life, which is what keeps a real curve climbing. (A one-hour run of the same stack, with a fifth of the parents, could not see the rise at all and looked flat: the error bars were as wide as the effect.)

The second is a lesson about the model, not the data. Fitted through the origin, the square-root line predicts that a tiny order costs nothing, but the smallest bucket still pays 0.44 bps, because even the smallest order crosses half a spread. Real pre-trade models carry that as a separate term, cost = spread/2 + C·σ·√(Q/ADV), and fitting through the origin forces fitC to compensate. That is one reason it comes out at 50 to 200 here, depending on the run, rather than near 1; the other is that σ here is print-to-print rather than daily. On a desk's HDB, with daily volatility, 30-day ADV and a spread term, the same query gives a constant you can compare with the literature.

Read it the way you read a markout curve: the points, not the line. If the realised points bend up faster than the model at the large end, your big orders are paying more than their size explains, which usually means they are being worked too fast. And the error bars matter, because the top bucket has the fewest orders and the most noise.

A second model: I-star

The square-root law has one constant. Kissell and Glantz's I-star model, which EDHEC works through in detail,9 has two parts. The instantaneous impact is what the whole order would cost if it hit the market at once, a power function of size and volatility. How much of it you actually pay then depends on how slowly you trade: about 95% of it is temporary and fades if you participate gently, and 5% is permanent and stays whatever you do.

\[ I_{\text{bps}} = a_1\,Z^{a_2}\,\sigma^{a_3},\qquad \text{cost}_{\text{bps}} = I\left(0.95\,\eta^{-1} + 0.05\right),\qquad \eta = 1+\frac{0.5}{\text{POV}} \]

Here \(Z\) is size as a percentage of ADV, \(\sigma\) daily volatility in bps, and POV the order's share of market volume while it trades. The fitted values below are EDHEC's, from Kissell and Glantz's data; refit them to your own flow before trusting them. The check reproduces EDHEC's worked example: 100,000 shares, 10% of ADV, 200 bps volatility, over a whole day.

The two models disagree on purpose. Square-root says cost depends on size; I-star says it depends on size and on how fast you go, which is what lets a pre-trade tool price a VWAP schedule and a 10% POV schedule differently for the same order.

Two cautions. Realised cost against arrival also carries drift that would have happened without you, so the fit is an upper bound on what you caused unless you remove the market's move first (F6). And the per-fill price impact in C2 is a different number. It is the mid's move after a single fill, and it mixes your impact with adverse selection; one fill's worth of evidence cannot separate them.

Strategy capacity. The same curve answers the question a portfolio manager asks before scaling up. Cost grows with order size relative to volume and with how often you trade, so somewhere there is a size at which expected cost eats the strategy's edge. The cost curve and I-star say where that size is.

The Algo Wheel

Companion · The Order & Trade Lifecycle: the routing problem the wheel and the smart order router are both answers to.

Everything so far produces numbers. This is where the numbers start steering. The point of measuring execution isn't a tidy monthly report — it's to make the next order cheaper. The algo wheel is the machine that turns yesterday's TCA into today's routing decision, and it closes the loop this whole document has been building toward.

1What an Algo Is

An execution algorithm — an "algo" — is an automated trader in a box. You hand it a goal, like "buy 100,000 AAPL by noon, stay under 10% of volume," and it decides everything underneath: how to slice the parent, when to release each child, and which venue to send it to. Different algos chase different goals — a VWAP algo tries to match the day's volume curve, an arrival algo trades urgently up front to beat the drift, a POV algo just holds a fixed share of volumePOV. Percentage-of-volume: the algo sizes its children to stay at, say, 10% of whatever the market trades — so it speeds up when volume does and eases off when it thins. — so the same order handed to two algos can cost very different amounts.

2The Problem: Which One, When?

A desk has many algos — and many brokers offering them — and the best choice depends on the order and the market. A tight, liquid, calm name rewards patience; a wide, jumpy one rewards urgency. The catch is that you can never run the same order two ways at once to see which was better, so for years the choice was habit, and nobody could prove the habit right.

3What the Wheel Does

An algo wheel is the fix.Why “wheel”. It began as a way to strip out trader bias when comparing brokers: assign orders by a fixed rota, not by preference, so every algo is judged on a fair, comparable sample. In its simplest form it is just a rule that spreads orders across the available algos — often partly at random — so each one gets a fair sample of comparable orders instead of only the orders a trader happened to trust it with. Then you score them with the TCA you've already built. The wheel "turns": algos that execute cheaply under the conditions they faced earn more of the flow, the ones that don't earn less. It is A/B testing for execution, run continuously.

Two different decisions, two different levels. The wheel chooses which algo gets the parent order — one decision, taken once, at the top. The algo's own smart order router (SOR) then chooses which venue gets each child slice — thousands of decisions, taken in microseconds, inside the broker. Both are measured by the same arithmetic, but from different tables and on different clocks: the wheel is judged on arrival slippage per algoOrderID (algoArrival[]), over days of flow; the SOR is judged on markout and spreads per venue, over individual fills. A venue scorecard that looks terrible can be an SOR problem inside an algo the wheel is right to keep — so read §Venue Scorecard before you reroute, and the wheel before you reweight.

4From Scorecard to Controller

Combine the contextual features from the Beyond-Vanilla labs and the scorecard stops reporting on the past and starts driving the routing: it becomes a controller. You now capture, for every fill, the conditions it faced — spread, order-flow imbalance, participation, venue, the order’s cohortCohort. Group orders by what you can’t control — liquidity tier, side, size bucket — so the wheel compares like with like, not a hard order against an easy one. — and the realised slippage it earned. Fit a model that predicts slippage from those conditions and its coefficients are the wheel's weights: they say which algo wins in which regime — and the same fit, run per fill with venue flags instead of algo flags, says the same about the SOR. The router reads them to assign the next order, measures the outcome, and updates. That is the loop in the diagram, made of arithmetic.

That is the honest output of an algo wheel. Random assignment reduces selection bias, and pairing removes the market, but the differences a desk cares about are still a few tenths of a basis point, and they take thousands of orders to resolve. A wheel that reweights on the first week’s averages is reweighting on noise; one that waits for the standard errors to shrink, and prefers paired comparisons where it has them, is not. drivers[] fits the same slippage on how each order was traded — passive share, venue mix, participation — and is the place to look for why an algo costs what it does, but not for which algo to choose: those are things the algo does, so they would soak up the very effect the wheel is trying to measure.

This is the whole argument of multi-dimensional TCA (Talos, Sparrow): the benchmarks were never meant to be more numbers for a report — they are the feedback the router reads. And the regression that fits them is one line of q, (inv flip[X] mmu X) mmu flip[X] mmu y, over the very same execs and quote tables every other lab in this document used.

Twelve Ways to Get This Wrong

These are scattered through the labs as warnings; collected here they make a checklist. Every one of them produces a number that is arithmetically correct and practically false — which is why they survive in production. All of them happen on real desks.

01

Using VWAP as the only benchmark

It asks whether you traded like everyone else, not whether you traded well. An order that is half the volume in a name is the VWAP, and beats it by construction. Pair it with arrival or it flatters your biggest orders most.

02

Quoting slippage without fill rate

+4 bps on 20% filled is a worse outcome than −1 bps on 98%, and the first number looks better in every report. fillRate belongs in the same select, every time.

03

Ranking venues by fill rate

The venue that fills you fastest is often the one filling you because the price is about to move. Fill rate measures availability; markout measures what that availability cost.

04

Reading one markout horizon

A single horizon cannot tell a faster trader from a better-informed one. Damage at 1s that recovers by 60s is a latency story; damage that compounds is an information story. It's the shape of the curve that tells you which.

05

Pooling internalised and lit flow

Internalised fills cross at the mid with no spread cost and, by construction, no venue markout. Blended into a lit scorecard they quietly improve every number and hide what the router is doing.

06

Averaging without the distribution

One bad print at −400 bps moves a venue's mean and leaves its median untouched. If mean and median disagree, you have a tail, not a trend — look before you reroute.

07

Ignoring opportunity cost

Every benchmark that scores only the fills rewards an algo for not trading. The unfilled remainder has a price; weight the drift by 1-fillRate and put it in the same table.

08

Mixing marketable and limited orders

A buy limited at $100 could never have traded through $100, so the prints above it don't belong in its benchmark. Scoring a limited order against the full-window VWAP compares it to a market it was never in.

09

Reconstructing book state afterwards

Spread, depth and queue position at the moment of placement are not recoverable from history — the book has moved on and your own order is gone from it. Stamp them on the ord row in-band or lose them.

10

Breaking point-in-time correctness

Score a 10:00:00 fill against a 10:00:02 mid and you have smuggled two seconds of hindsight into the number. In kdb+ this is the difference between aj and an ordinary join, and it is silent.

11

Counting every print in VWAP

A real tape carries odd lots, opening and closing auction prints, out-of-sequence reports and late trades, each flagged with a condition code. Leave them in and your VWAP includes trades you could never have joined; the auction print alone can be a fifth of the day. Filter on an eligibility flag set once at ingest (§Production Notes). This stack's synthetic tape has no conditions, which is exactly why the mistake is easy to carry into production.

12

Adding up the children's spreads

Each child's effective spread is measured against the mid at its own fill, and that mid has already been pushed up by the children before it. Sum them and the parent looks cheap: every slice paid a fair spread on a price the order itself had moved. A parent's cost is measured from one price, the arrival mid, across all its fills (B1), never as the sum of its children's.

.b.ivwap filters the tape once per parent: clear and correct, but it does work proportional to parents × window length. On a full day of a real tape, the better shape is running sums. Five Rules

If you remember nothing else, remember these. Each is the general form of several of the mistakes above, and each is argued in full somewhere in the labs.

1

Arrival first, VWAP second

VWAP asks whether you traded like everyone else; arrival asks whether you traded well. An order that is a large share of the volume is the VWAP, so the biggest orders flatter it most. Lead with arrival and opportunity cost; show VWAP beside them, never instead of them. And expect any benchmark a desk is paid on to be gamed: arrival is the hardest to game, because it is fixed before you trade.

2

Point-in-time, always

Every benchmark reads a price that was knowable at the moment being measured. aj, never a plain join. Getting this wrong raises no error. Join a fill to a quote stamped after it and the query still runs and returns a plausible number; it is just measured against a price you couldn't have seen when you traded.

3

Cost never travels alone

Slippage beside fill rate, the mean beside the median, and one sign convention in every table you publish. A venue gets a row, not a grade: fill rate, markout, realised spread and fee each say something different. A number with nothing beside it is a number someone will misread.

4

Curves, not points

Markouts across horizons, volume across the day, cost across size. One horizon cannot tell being outrun from being outguessed; one average cannot tell a trend from a tail.

5

Measure to change tomorrow

A scorecard nobody routes on is a report. TCA earns its keep only when today's numbers move tomorrow's flow: the wheel reweighting algos, the router reweighting venues, an alert reaching a trader while the order is still working.

The first four are versions of one rule: a benchmark is a claim about a counterfactual, what a fair execution would have cost, and a counterfactual is only as honest as the information you let into it. Arrival over VWAP, point-in-time joins, qualifiers beside every number and curves instead of averages are four ways of refusing to let hindsight, convention or aggregation do the arguing for you. The fifth is the reason to bother.

F4The PWP Range — a 2-D Benchmark

Run in TCA

PWP (participation-weighted price) measures in volume-time: for a 20% target, the benchmark is the VWAP of the first Q/0.20 shares the market printed after you started. That is the opportunity set you traded against.

Sparrow's move: don't stop at the single number. Over that same volume, compute the best and worst average price an order your size could have got — the cheapest Q shares and the dearest Q. Now you have a range, and the real question: where in it did you land?

0 = you achieved the best possible fill for your size over the opportunity set; 1 = the worst. A PWP slippage of +2 bps means little until you know the range was 40 bps wide.

/ beyond.q — PWP, the achievable range, and where each parent landed
/ size-weighted average of the first Q shares of a price-sorted tape
.b.takeQ:{[v;Q] cum:sums v`size; k:1+first where cum>=Q;
  w:k#v`size; w[k-1]-:(sum w)-Q;                    / trim the last lot to hit Q exactly
  w wavg k#v`px };

/ PWP plus the cheapest and dearest average an order of Q shares could have got
.b.pwpRange:{[trade;tenor;sy;startTime;Q]
  v:select px,size from trade where sym=sy, time>=startTime;

    (v`size) wavg v`px;
    .b.takeQ[`px xasc v; Q];
    .b.takeQ[`px xdesc v; Q]) };

F8Live Exceptions

Run in TCA

A strategy is backtested with a cost in it: two bps of slippage, a 95% fill rate. Those are model assumptions like any other, and when live execution drifts away from them the strategy looks as if its signal is decaying. Retire it then and you have retired a good signal for an execution problem.

The fix is to watch execution against its own expectations while the day is still running. Every two minutes, when a window of algo orders completes, the CEP scores each algo once and appends to alerts only if it sits more than three robust standard deviations from where it usually sits. The table stays empty until something needs a human, which is the difference between monitoring and a dashboard nobody reads.

Four choices keep the alarm honest:

  • Windows, not orders, are the observations. Orders from the same parent and minute move together; counting them as independent turns noise into three-sigma events.
  • Each algo is measured against its peers in the same window, so a move the whole market makes cancels out.
  • Each window is scored once, when it completes, not every time the timer fires.
  • "Usual" is the median and the median absolute deviation, so a few wild windows (dark fills have heavy tails) can't widen the yardstick. And nothing alerts until ten windows of history exist.

Each choice was learned from a run that broke the version before it:

  1. Orders as observations (15 min): BATS and EDGX flagged every ten seconds, in a simulation where nothing changes.
  2. + windows (1 h): 15 algo and 78 venue alerts, in two bursts with all six venues together. The market moved, not a venue.
  3. + peer-relative (4.5 h): 21 algo alerts, 16 of them DARK, and 11 venue alerts.
  4. + once per window, median (3 h): 3 and 3. Four of the six came in the first twenty minutes, so it now waits for ten windows.

Two baselines are better than one. exceptions[] asks whether an algo has changed since this morning. vsModel asks whether its orders cost more than their size says they should, using the square-root estimate from §Market Impact. The industry calls the gap between a pre-trade estimate and the realised cost the value add, and ranks brokers and algos by it. In a 60-minute run it put ARRIVAL at z = 5.7, costing 0.96 bps against 0.40 predicted. That isn't an execution problem but the model's blind spot: arrival algos trade fast, and a model that only knows size can't see speed, which is the gap I-star closes (§Market Impact).

The router gets the same treatment: venueAlerts flags a venue whose realised spread drifts away from the other venues'.

A session baseline cannot see a day that starts badly. In production, the baseline is the wheel's history for that algo and the venue's history on the scorecard, carried over from the HDB.

EDHEC sorts every benchmark two ways: when it is fixed (before, during or after the trade) and whether it depends on the time of the trade. Six are still common in client reports, and each has a function here: yesterday's close, the open, the available VWAP (open to arrival, nothing after), LHOC (the mean of the day's low, high, open and close), the high–low midpoint, and the close.

The first three were knowable when the order arrived; the last three know the whole day, so they score a trader on information the trader did not have. The available VWAP reuses the running sums from §Production Notes: one aj at each parent's arrival.

With one simulated session there is no yesterday, so the wrapper passes refPx, the feed's anchor prices, as the previous close. On a real desk that is the prior day's close from the HDB.

  a:aj[`sym`time; a; update `g#sym from select sym,time,cn,cv from c];
  a:a lj select open:first px, high:max px, low:min px, close:last px by sym from trade;
  a:update phi:.b.buyorsell side, prevClose:prevCl sym, availVwap:cn%cv,
           lhoc:0.25*low+high+open+close, midHL:0.5*high+low from a;

    vsPrevClose:f[phi;prevClose;averageFillPrice], vsOpen:f[phi;open;averageFillPrice],
    vsAvailVwap:f[phi;availVwap;averageFillPrice], vsLHOC:f[phi;lhoc;averageFillPrice],
    vsMidHL:f[phi;midHL;averageFillPrice], vsClose:f[phi;close;averageFillPrice] from a };

q)select n:count i, prevClose:avg vsPrevClose, open:avg vsOpen, availVwap:avg vsAvailVwap,
